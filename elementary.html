Elementary–Middle School Curriculum

An 8-week introductory program designed for complete beginners. No prior coding or typing experience assumed. Each session runs 45–60 minutes.

Weekly Session Format (45–60 Minutes)

5 mins Recap of last week's vocabulary word(s)
5–10 mins Introduce the day's idea with a real-world example
25–35 mins Hands-on building time following the step-by-step guide
5–10 mins Test on the micro:bit and share with a partner

Learning Goals

  • Understand that a computer only does what it is told to do, one instruction at a time.
  • Learn the basic parts of a computer: mouse, keyboard, and web browser.
  • Learn the parts of a micro:bit: the 25 LED lights, Button A, Button B, and the USB connector.
  • Open the MakeCode website and recognize the three main parts of the editor screen.

Materials Needed

  • One computer or tablet per 1–2 students
  • One micro:bit board per 1–2 students (not required this week)
  • A projector or shared screen for demonstrations

Step-by-Step Lesson

  1. Explain that a computer needs step-by-step instructions to do anything, the same way a recipe tells you exactly what to do to bake a cake. These instructions are called "code."
  2. Pass around a micro:bit board (or show it on the projector). Point out the grid of small lights (called LEDs), the two buttons labeled A and B, and the connector where a cable plugs in.
  3. Have students open a web browser on their computer.
  4. Tell students to type the address makecode.microbit.org into the address bar at the top of the browser window and press Enter.
  5. Show students how to click the button labeled "New Project" and give the project a name, such as their own name.
  6. Point out the three main areas of the screen: the list of block categories on the left side, the empty workspace in the middle where blocks are put together, and the picture of a micro:bit on the right side (called the simulator) that shows what the code would do.
  7. Demonstrate clicking on the "Basic" category, finding the block called "show icon," and dragging it into the middle workspace so it snaps underneath the block that is already there.
  8. Let every student try dragging that same block into their own project and watch the heart picture appear on the simulator on the right.
  9. Give students a few minutes of free practice dragging different blocks in and out of the workspace, just to get comfortable with the mouse.

Optional Challenge (for students who finish early)

Let students try a few different icons from the "show icon" block and guess what each picture is before running it.

Wrap-Up

Ask the group: "What does a computer need in order to do something?" Listen for answers like "instructions" or "code."

Learning Goals

  • Write and run a first real program on the micro:bit.
  • Understand that a set of instructions in order is called a program.
  • Learn how to move a finished program from the computer onto the micro:bit itself.

Materials Needed

  • One computer per 1–2 students
  • One micro:bit board per 1–2 students
  • One USB cable per micro:bit

Step-by-Step Lesson

  1. Quickly review last week's words: computer, code, program.
  2. Have students open makecode.microbit.org and start a new project called "Name Badge."
  3. In the "Basic" category, find the block called "show string."
  4. Drag "show string" so it snaps underneath the "on start" block that is already in the workspace.
  5. Click on the quotation marks inside the "show string" block and type in your own name.
  6. Watch the simulator on the right scroll your name across the lights.
  7. From the "Basic" category, drag a "forever" block into the workspace, then move the "show string" block inside it, so the name repeats over and over instead of showing just once.
  8. Click the "Download" button at the bottom of the screen. Explain that this saves a small file with the instructions for the micro:bit.
  9. Plug the micro:bit into the computer with the USB cable. A drive will appear on the computer named MICROBIT, the same way a USB flash drive would.
  10. Drag the downloaded file onto the MICROBIT drive (or, on some computers, it copies automatically). Watch the yellow light on the back of the micro:bit blink and then stop — this means the program has been saved onto the board.
  11. Unplug the micro:bit (or leave it plugged in) and watch your name scroll across the lights.

Optional Challenge (for students who finish early)

Try changing the message to a favorite word or a short greeting instead of just a name.

Wrap-Up

Have each student wear or hold up their badge and show it to a partner.

Learning Goals

  • Understand that a sequence is a list of steps done in a specific order.
  • Understand that a loop repeats the same steps without retyping them.
  • Create a simple two-frame animation.

Materials Needed

  • One computer per 1–2 students
  • One micro:bit board per 1–2 students
  • One USB cable per micro:bit

Step-by-Step Lesson

  1. Ask students how a flip-book of drawings makes a picture look like it's moving (showing many still pictures quickly, one after another).
  2. Start a new project called "Beating Heart."
  3. In the "Basic" category, find the block called "show leds," which shows a blank grid that can be clicked on to light up individual dots.
  4. Drag a "show leds" block under "on start" and click on the grid squares to draw a large heart shape.
  5. From "Basic," drag a "pause (ms)" block underneath, and leave the time at 100 milliseconds for now.
  6. Add a second "show leds" block after the pause, and draw a slightly smaller heart shape in it.
  7. Add one more "pause (ms)" block after the second heart.
  8. Run it in the simulator. Notice that it only plays once and then stops.
  9. Drag a "forever" block into the workspace, then move the entire sequence (both hearts and both pauses) inside the forever block, so it repeats endlessly, like a real heartbeat.
  10. Download the program and copy it onto the micro:bit as done in Week 2.

Optional Challenge (for students who finish early)

Try adding a third, even smaller heart shape in the middle for a smoother-looking beat.

Wrap-Up

Ask the group to explain in their own words what a "loop" is, using the heartbeat as the example.

Learning Goals

  • Understand what an input is (something the computer senses, like a button press).
  • Understand what an output is (something the computer shows or does in response).
  • Use both buttons on the micro:bit to control what is shown on the lights.

Materials Needed

  • One computer per 1–2 students
  • One micro:bit board per 1–2 students
  • One USB cable per micro:bit

Step-by-Step Lesson

  1. Point to Button A and Button B on the micro:bit and remind students what they look like.
  2. Start a new project called "Emotion Badge."
  3. In the "Input" category, find the block called "on button A pressed."
  4. Inside that block, place a "show icon" block and choose a happy face.
  5. From "Input," drag a second block called "on button B pressed" into the workspace.
  6. Inside that block, place another "show icon" block, but this time choose a sad face.
  7. Explain that pressing a button is an input, and the picture that appears is an output — the computer is responding to what it senses.
  8. Download the program to the micro:bit and test it by physically pressing Button A, then Button B, and watching the faces change.
  9. Let students replace the happy and sad faces with any two icons they like.

Optional Challenge (for students who finish early)

Add a third feeling by having both buttons pressed at the same time trigger a different icon, using the block "on button A+B pressed."

Wrap-Up

Have each student demonstrate their two "emotions" to the group by pressing each button.

Learning Goals

  • Understand what a variable is: a labeled place that stores a number and can change.
  • Use the accelerometer (motion sensor) as an input.
  • Understand why the order of instructions matters.

Materials Needed

  • One computer per 1–2 students
  • One micro:bit board per 1–2 students
  • One USB cable per micro:bit

Step-by-Step Lesson

  1. Explain that a variable is like a labeled box that holds a number, and that number can change as the program runs.
  2. Start a new project called "Step Counter."
  3. In the "Variables" category, click "Make a Variable," and name it "steps."
  4. Under the "on start" block, drag a "set steps to 0" block so the count begins at zero.
  5. In the "Input" category, find the block called "on shake."
  6. Inside "on shake," place a "change steps by 1" block, which adds one to the variable every time the micro:bit is shaken.
  7. Still inside "on shake," add a "show number steps" block after the "change steps by 1" block, so the current count is displayed after it changes.
  8. Ask the group: what would happen if "show number" were placed before "change steps by 1" instead of after? (The display would always be one step behind.) Try it both ways to see the difference.
  9. Download the program to the micro:bit.
  10. Put the micro:bit in a pocket or hold it and shake it gently a few times, then press it or check the display to see the step count.

Optional Challenge (for students who finish early)

Add a "reset" feature using "on button A pressed" to set steps back to 0.

Wrap-Up

Ask students how a real pedometer or fitness tracker might use the same idea (motion as the input, a variable to count, and a display as the output).

Learning Goals

  • Understand "if / then / else" decision-making in code.
  • Use the micro:bit's built-in light sensor as an input.
  • Practice testing and adjusting a program to work in the real room.

Materials Needed

  • One computer per 1–2 students
  • One micro:bit board per 1–2 students
  • One USB cable per micro:bit

Step-by-Step Lesson

  1. Ask students what makes a real nightlight turn on by itself (it senses darkness).
  2. Start a new project called "Nightlight."
  3. In the "Input" category, find the "light level" block, which reports how bright or dark it is on a scale from 0 to 255.
  4. In the "Logic" category, drag an "if / then / else" block into a "forever" block from the "Basic" category.
  5. In the Logic category, find a comparison block (two boxes with a < symbol) and place it in the "if" condition slot.
  6. Drag the "light level" block into the left side of the comparison, and type a number like 30 into the right side, so it reads "light level < 30."
  7. In the "then" section, add a "show icon" with a light-bulb picture (or turn on all the LEDs using "plot" blocks).
  8. In the "else" section, add a "clear screen" block so the lights stay off when it's bright.
  9. Download the program to the micro:bit.
  10. Test it by cupping a hand over the front of the micro:bit to make it dark, and watch the light appear. Cover and uncover it a few times.
  11. As a class, adjust the number 30 up or down together until the light turns on at the right darkness for your room. Explain that this process — testing and adjusting — is exactly what real engineers do.

Optional Challenge (for students who finish early)

Add a third light level using "else if" for a dim, in-between setting.

Wrap-Up

Ask the group to think of another everyday example of "if / then / else," such as "if it is raining, then bring an umbrella, else leave it at home."

Learning Goals

  • Understand what randomness means in a program.
  • Combine several ideas learned so far: inputs, variables, and if/then logic.
  • Discuss whether a computer's random choice is fair.

Materials Needed

  • One computer per 1–2 students
  • One micro:bit board per 1–2 students
  • One USB cable per micro:bit

Step-by-Step Lesson

  1. Play one round of rock-paper-scissors with the whole group using hands, as a warm-up.
  2. Start a new project called "RPS."
  3. In the "Variables" category, make a new variable called "choice."
  4. In the "Input" category, drag an "on shake" block into the workspace.
  5. In the "Math" category, find the block "pick random 0 to 2."
  6. Inside "on shake," place a "set choice to" block, and put the "pick random 0 to 2" block into it.
  7. In the "Logic" category, drag an "if / then" block into "on shake," and click the small plus/gear icon on it to add an "else if" section, so there are three sections in total.
  8. Set the first condition to "choice = 0" and show a rock icon inside it.
  9. Set the second condition ("else if") to "choice = 1" and show a paper icon inside it.
  10. Leave the final "else" section to show a scissors icon, since that covers whatever is left (choice = 2).
  11. Download the program to the micro:bit and test it by shaking it a few times to see different results appear.

Optional Challenge (for students who finish early)

Have the whole class shake their micro:bits at the same time and compare results in a mini tournament.

Wrap-Up

Ask the group: does the computer pick rock, paper, or scissors evenly, or does it favor one? Why does a fair game need a random number?

Learning Goals

  • Review and reinforce the vocabulary and concepts learned across the program.
  • Personalize and present a favorite project.
  • Celebrate progress made over the 8 weeks.

Materials Needed

  • Computers and micro:bits from previous weeks
  • Index cards or paper with the words: input, output, loop, variable, if/then (optional)
  • Optional: simple certificates or stickers

Step-by-Step Lesson

  1. Play a quick review game: hold up a vocabulary word (input, output, loop, variable, if/then) and ask students to name a project from the last seven weeks that used it.
  2. Give students 15–20 minutes of free-build time to open their favorite project from Weeks 2–7 and personalize it — a new message, new icons, or new colors.
  3. Set up a "gallery walk": place all the finished micro:bits at different spots around the room, running their programs.
  4. Have students walk around in small groups to see everyone's projects in action.
  5. Ask each student to explain their favorite project to the group in one or two sentences: what it does and how it works.
  6. If possible, hand out a small certificate or recognition for completing the program.

Wrap-Up

Ask the group to share what surprised them most about coding, or what they'd like to try next.
